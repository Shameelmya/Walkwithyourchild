## appendix | For professionals: using this book, and caring for yourself

This appendix is for doctors, nurses, social workers, therapists, counsellors and volunteers who give this book to families.

### Using this book with families

- **Introduce it at diagnosis, not at the end.** The book follows the illness trajectory. Point families to the part that fits their stage, and return to it at each review.
- **Do not hand it over and leave.** Read a chapter together, especially Chapters 2, 3 and 8. For parents who do not read easily, read aloud or use the translated version.
- **Use the notebook in clinic.** Ask families to bring it. Review the medicines chart, seizure diary and questions at every visit, and fill in the main contact and emergency plan before discharge.
- **Add what is specific to the child.** The care chapters are deliberately general. Add condition-specific guidance (for example DMD, SMA, SSPE, Wilson's disease, metabolic disorders, leukodystrophies) and the child's own emergency plan.
- **Watch for the moments the book cannot hold:** a parent with thoughts of self-harm, a child in uncontrolled pain, a family in financial crisis, a sibling in distress. These need a person, not a page.
- **Speak without blame.** Families in this study were often blamed, by relatives and sometimes unintentionally by professionals. Explain genes to both parents together, and never in a way that singles out the mother.

### Caring for yourself

Professionals in this study described carrying the families' suffering: heartbreak in clinic, crying after a child's death, a heaviness taken home at night, and helplessness when systems block help. This is a **parallel process**: you may feel what the families feel. It is a normal response to this work, not a weakness.

| When | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| **Every day** | Pause between hard conversations. Mark the end of the workday with something that is yours: a walk, reading, prayer, a call home. |
| **Before a grief session** | Check your own state. If you are depleted, postpone an intensive grief conversation to the next day where it is safe to do so. |
| **After a child's death** | Debrief with the team. Let a colleague step in for death care if you cannot. Allow yourself time to recover. |
| **Weekly or monthly** | Peer case discussion and supervision with a senior colleague. |
| **Your caseload** | Where possible, mix progressive cases with other work. A varied caseload protects against exhaustion. |
| **Boundaries** | Notice when a child reminds you of your own child, niece or nephew. Stay warm and present without carrying the family home with you. |
| **Meaning** | Remember the difference you make; one physician asks, "What if I was not there?" Let meaning sustain you without turning into self-sacrifice. |

> [!care]
> **Warning signs of compassion fatigue:** feeling numb or detached, dreading work, a heaviness that does not lift after rest, irritability, poor sleep, or thinking of leaving clinical work. Talk to a supervisor or colleague you trust. Tele-MANAS (14416) is available to professionals too.

**What services owe their staff:** structured debriefing after deaths, regular supervision, continuing education in communication and grief work, and clear roles for social workers, counsellors and volunteers. *One person alone cannot do anything*: care for these families is team work, and so is caring for the team.

### Recognising developmental regression

A child whose symptoms began before the age of 16 is considered to have progressive intellectual and neurological deterioration when **all three** of the following are present: progressive deterioration for more than three months; loss of intellectual or developmental abilities already attained; and the development of abnormal neurological signs. Static losses, for example after encephalitis, head injury or near-drowning, are excluded.

| | Developmental regression | Developmental delay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pattern** | Loss of skills already acquired | Skills not acquired at the expected age |
| **Trajectory** | Declining function | Slower but steady progress |
| **Onset** | After a period of normal development | From early infancy |
| **Outlook** | Often progressive; may be life-limiting | Static or slowly improving |
| **Usual cause** | Often a degenerative disease | Congenital or acquired early |

### Main groups of conditions

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Lysosomal storage disorders** | NCL (Batten disease), Gaucher, Niemann-Pick type C, GM1 and GM2 gangliosidosis, Krabbe, MPS |
| **Leukodystrophies** | Metachromatic leukodystrophy, X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy, Canavan, Alexander, Pelizaeus-Merzbacher |
| **Mitochondrial disorders** | Leigh syndrome, MERRF, MELAS, complex I–IV deficiencies, coenzyme Q10 disorders |
| **Brain iron accumulation (NBIA)** | PKAN, PLAN, MPAN, BPAN, Kufor-Rakeb, Woodhouse-Sakati |
| **Peroxisomal disorders** | Zellweger spectrum, neonatal adrenoleukodystrophy, Refsum disease, RCDP |
| **Organic acid and amino acid disorders** | Glutaric aciduria type I, maple syrup urine disease, serine deficiency |
| **Metal metabolism disorders** | Wilson disease, SLC30A10 manganese deposition, Menkes disease |
| **Progressive myoclonus epilepsies** | Lafora, sialidosis, Unverricht-Lundborg, NCL variants (CLN1–CLN14) |

White matter disease usually presents with gait disturbance, spasticity, behavioural change and falling school performance; grey matter disease with seizures, cognitive decline and visual loss. Early signs to ask about: loss of motor milestones, hypotonia or spasticity, ataxia, dystonia; stagnation, loss of speech, personality change; loss of vision or hearing; and new tonic-clonic, myoclonic or absence seizures. The usual course runs through five stages: pre-symptomatic, plateau, active regression, severe disability and end stage. Palliative care belongs in every one of them, not only the last.

### Frameworks that guide this work

**Paediatric palliative care** is an active, total approach to the child's body, mind and spirit, beginning at diagnosis and continuing throughout the illness, with the family as the unit of care (WHO, 2018). In neurological conditions it includes seizure palliation and home rescue plans, comfort-focused care of dystonia and spasticity, feeding and gastrostomy decisions, secretion and breathing care, pain assessment with non-verbal scales such as FLACC and r-FLACC, and psychosocial care: advance care planning, memory-making, sibling support and bereavement follow-up.

- **IAPC standards for India (2025):** ten domains, namely symptom assessment and management, drug availability, care of the dying child, transition of care, perinatal and neonatal care, community-based care, education and training, ethics and law, policy and governance, and research and quality.
- **Core outcome domains** for children with severe neurological impairment and their families (Ribbers et al., 2019): symptom control, respite and support, normalcy, empowerment, security, and coping with disease.
- **Family Systems Illness Model** (Rolland, 1994): illness is a stressor for the whole family. Consider the type of illness (most of these have a gradual onset, a progressive course, increasing incapacity and a life-shortening outcome), its time phases (crisis, chronic, terminal), and family functioning: beliefs and meaning-making, roles, communication, earlier losses across generations, and fit with the family's life cycle.
- **Other useful lenses:** the biopsychosocial-spiritual model, Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems, the dual process model of grief (Stroebe and Schut), and a strengths-based approach.

### The psychiatric social worker across levels of care

| Level | Interventions |
| --- | --- |
| **Community and home** | Family psychoeducation and carer skills; links with ASHA and Anganwadi workers and volunteers; grief support; school liaison for siblings; referral pathways; mobilising NGOs, faith groups and local networks |
| **Hospital and hospice** | Psychosocial assessment with standard tools; goals-of-care discussions; discharge and transition planning; advance care planning in line with the family's values; anticipatory grief and ethical distress; financial aid, insurance and welfare schemes |
| **Specialist palliative team** | Complex case management; ethical consultation and shared decisions on withholding or withdrawing treatment; structured bereavement programmes; end-of-life coordination, memory-making and place-of-death planning; training, research and policy advocacy |

Explain genetic risk to both parents together and without blame, and pace any conversation about future pregnancies to the family's readiness.
