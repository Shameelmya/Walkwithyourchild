## appendix | For professionals: using this book, and caring for yourself

This appendix is for doctors, nurses, social workers, therapists, counsellors and volunteers who give this book to families.

### Using this book with families

- **Introduce it at diagnosis, not at the end.** The book follows the illness trajectory. Point families to the part that fits their stage, and return to it at each review.
- **Do not hand it over and leave.** Read a chapter together, especially Chapters 2, 3 and 8. For parents who do not read easily, read aloud or use the translated version.
- **Use the notebook in clinic.** Ask families to bring it. Review the medicines chart, seizure diary and questions at every visit, and fill in the main contact and emergency plan before discharge.
- **Add what is specific to the child.** The care chapters are deliberately general. Add condition-specific guidance (for example DMD, SMA, SSPE, Wilson's disease, metabolic disorders, leukodystrophies) and the child's own emergency plan.
- **Watch for the moments the book cannot hold:** a parent with thoughts of self-harm, a child in uncontrolled pain, a family in financial crisis, a sibling in distress. These need a person, not a page.
- **Speak without blame.** Families in this study were often blamed, by relatives and sometimes unintentionally by professionals. Explain genes to both parents together, and never in a way that singles out the mother.

### Caring for yourself

Professionals in this study described carrying the families' suffering: heartbreak in clinic, crying after a child's death, a heaviness taken home at night, and helplessness when systems block help. This is a **parallel process**: you may feel what the families feel. It is a normal response to this work, not a weakness.

| When | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| **Every day** | Pause between hard conversations. Mark the end of the workday with something that is yours: a walk, reading, prayer, a call home. |
| **Before a grief session** | Check your own state. If you are depleted, postpone an intensive grief conversation to the next day where it is safe to do so. |
| **After a child's death** | Debrief with the team. Let a colleague step in for death care if you cannot. Allow yourself time to recover. |
| **Weekly or monthly** | Peer case discussion and supervision with a senior colleague. |
| **Your caseload** | Where possible, mix progressive cases with other work. A varied caseload protects against exhaustion. |
| **Boundaries** | Notice when a child reminds you of your own child, niece or nephew. Stay warm and present without carrying the family home with you. |
| **Meaning** | Remember the difference you make; one physician asks, "What if I was not there?" Let meaning sustain you without turning into self-sacrifice. |

> [!care]
> **Warning signs of compassion fatigue:** feeling numb or detached, dreading work, a heaviness that does not lift after rest, irritability, poor sleep, or thinking of leaving clinical work. Talk to a supervisor or colleague you trust. Tele-MANAS (14416) is available to professionals too.

**What services owe their staff:** structured debriefing after deaths, regular supervision, continuing education in communication and grief work, and clear roles for social workers, counsellors and volunteers. *One person alone cannot do anything*: care for these families is team work, and so is caring for the team.
