## chapter:0 | Before you begin

**This book is for you if your child has a neurological condition that is changing over time,** and the doctors have told you it cannot be fully cured. It is for mothers, fathers, grandparents and anyone who cares for the child at home.

You may be holding this book on a hospital bench, on a long train journey, or at home late at night. You may be tired. You do not need to read it all at once.

### How to use this book

- **Read the part that fits where you are now.** The book follows the journey many families walk: from the first signs, to living with the condition, to the time when the illness advances, and beyond. Turn to the part you need today.
- **Use the notebook at the end.** It has pages for your child's details, your questions for the doctor, a medicines chart, an emergency card and more. Take it to every hospital visit.
- **Read it with someone.** Your partner, a family member, a nurse or the social worker can read with you. Many parents find it easier that way.
- **This book does not replace your child's doctors.** It helps you understand, prepare and ask good questions. Whenever you see **"Ask your team"**, take that question to the doctors, nurses or social worker caring for your child.

### You did not cause this

Many of these conditions are genetic. That means they come from tiny instructions inside the body's cells, called genes. Every child gets genes from both parents.

When a gene change causes an illness, **it is no one's fault.** It is not the mother's fault. It is not the father's fault. It is not a punishment for anything anyone did in this life or a past one. No parent chooses the genes they pass on, and no parent can see them.

If anyone in your family blames you, you can show them this page. You can also ask the doctor or social worker to explain it to the whole family together.

### You are not alone

The families who helped make this book live in villages, towns and cities across India. They speak many languages. Some have one child who is ill; some have lost a child before. They told us about the long journeys, the debts, the sleepless nights, the neighbours' words, and also about their child's laughter, music, drawings and love.

The doctors, nurses, therapists and social workers who helped make this book told us something too: **parents who understand what is happening and are supported become remarkably skilled carers.** You already know your child better than anyone. This book is here to walk beside you.

> [!note]
> *A note on the stories in this book:* the parent experiences described here are drawn from many families and changed so that no one can be recognised.

# part:1 | When something seems wrong

## chapter:1 | The search for a name

For many families, the hardest early months are spent not knowing. Your child was growing well, and then something changed. Perhaps a small jerk after a fever, eyes rolling upward, falling more often, losing words, or no longer sitting up. You went to the doctor. Then another. Perhaps you were told, "Don't worry, the child will catch up." Perhaps you were told something that later turned out to be wrong.

If this has happened to you, you did nothing wrong by trusting what you were told. These conditions are rare. Many doctors see them only a few times in their working life. The early signs can be small and look like ordinary childhood illness. Some tests, especially genetic tests, take weeks or months.

### Why it can take so long

- **The signs are often small at first,** and only become clear over months.
- **There are thousands of rare conditions,** and many look alike in the beginning.
- **The right test may only be available in a few large hospitals.**
- **Genetic results can come back "uncertain".** This means the test found a change in a gene, but science does not yet know whether that change causes illness. It is not a mistake by you or the hospital. Ask what it means for your child, and whether more tests are planned.

### What you can do

1. **Keep one folder for everything.** Put every report, scan, prescription and discharge summary in it, in date order. Carry it to every visit.
2. **Record what you see.** If your child has unusual movements, staring spells or jerks, take a short video on your phone. Note the date, the time and how long it lasted. Doctors find this very helpful.
3. **Write down the changes.** What could your child do six months ago that they cannot do now? Use the "My child" page in the notebook.
4. **Ask for a referral if things are not getting clearer.** You can say: "My child is getting worse and we still don't know why. Can you refer us to a child neurologist?"
5. **Be careful with promises.** If a clinic promises a cure for a large sum of money, pause. Read Chapter 4 before you pay.

### Before a long journey to a big hospital

Many families travel for days to reach a specialist centre. A little preparation can save money and stress.

- **Ask if an appointment is needed,** and how long you may need to stay.
- **Check your documents** (see Chapter 8). Some state health cards are not accepted in hospitals in another state. Ask before you travel.
- **Ask if anyone near your home can continue the care** after the visit, so you do not have to return for every small problem.
- **If possible, take someone with you,** so one person can rest, eat or stand in queues while the other stays with your child.

### How you may feel

You may feel exhausted, frustrated, angry with doctors who missed things, or guilty that you did not come sooner. These feelings are common and understandable. Searching for an answer for your child is an act of love. You have already shown great strength by getting this far.

> [!ask]
> - "What tests have been done, and what are we still waiting for?"
> - "When will we know the results, and who will explain them to us?"
> - "Is there a doctor closer to our home who can work with you?"

## chapter:2 | Understanding the diagnosis

When the diagnosis finally comes, it often comes with long words and in a busy ward. Many parents told us they nodded their heads but did not understand. Some were afraid to ask because the doctors spoke in English or used medical words. **You have every right to understand what is happening to your child.** Asking questions is not rude. It is part of caring for your child.

### Questions to ask

Copy these into your notebook and take them to the doctor. You do not need to ask them all at once.

- What is the name of my child's condition? Can you write it down for me?
- What is happening inside my child's body?
- How is it likely to change over the coming months and years?
- Which parts can be treated or eased, even if the condition cannot be cured?
- What should I watch for at home? What is an emergency?
- Whom do I call if I have a question after we go home?
- Are there other families with this condition we could talk to?

### If you do not understand

- **Ask for your language.** You can say: "Please explain in Hindi / Kannada / Malayalam / Bengali / Tamil," or ask if a nurse, social worker or interpreter can help.
- **Say it back.** "Let me tell you what I understood, so you can correct me." This is one of the best ways to catch misunderstandings.
- **Bring someone with you** to listen and take notes.
- **Ask again.** Understanding a serious diagnosis takes more than one conversation. Good doctors expect to explain things several times.
- **Nurses and social workers can help.** Many parents find it easier to ask them. They can also arrange a time to talk with the doctor.

### Genes, in plain words

Every cell of the body carries instructions, called genes. A child gets one set from the mother and one set from the father. Sometimes a small change in one gene stops the body from working as it should.

- **Some conditions happen when both parents carry the same hidden change.** The parents are healthy and usually never know. Each pregnancy then has a chance of being affected.
- **Some conditions are passed through the mother's side,** because of the way some genes are carried. The mother is usually healthy and has no way of knowing.
- **Some gene changes happen for the first time in the child,** and neither parent carries them.

In every case, **no one chose this, no one could see it, and no one is to blame.** Carrying a gene change is common and invisible. If relatives say the illness came from one parent's "bad blood", ask the doctor or genetic counsellor to explain to the whole family together.

### Tests and consent

- **You can ask what a test will cost before you agree to it,** and whether any research funding or free scheme covers it.
- **Ask what the result will change.** Will it change treatment, or help with planning future pregnancies?
- **Genetic tests should be done in a reliable, accredited laboratory.** Ask your doctor which laboratory to use.
- **Ask who will explain the result,** and when. Some parents never hear the result of a test they paid for. You have the right to know.

### Future children and other family members

Because many of these conditions are genetic, the doctor may suggest a meeting with a **genetic counsellor**. This is someone trained to explain how the condition runs in families. They can tell you the chance of it happening in another pregnancy, whether brothers, sisters or cousins should be tested, and what tests are possible early in a future pregnancy. This conversation is for you. It does not mean anyone is being blamed.

### What "palliative care" means

You may hear the words **palliative care** or **supportive care**. Many people think this means the child is dying soon. It does not. Palliative care means a team that helps your child feel as comfortable as possible and supports your whole family, **from the time of diagnosis and for as long as you need it,** alongside all other treatment. Many children receive this care for years.

> [!ask]
> - "Can you draw or show me what is happening?"
> - "Can we meet a genetic counsellor?"
> - "Is there a palliative or supportive care team here?"

## chapter:3 | Hearing hard news

There is no easy way to hear that your child has a condition that will not go away. Some parents remember every word. Others remember nothing after the first sentence. Many say the first question in their mind was: *Is there any cure?* And the second: *Then what is the point of everything we are doing?*

This chapter is about the days and weeks after that news.

### What you may feel

There is no right or wrong way to feel. Parents have told us about:

- **Shock and numbness** – going through the day as if in a dream.
- **Disbelief** – "The reports must be wrong. Another hospital will say something different."
- **Anger** – at doctors, at God, at your partner, at yourself, even at your child.
- **Guilt** – "Did I do something? Should I have come earlier?"
- **Bargaining** – "If I do everything right, maybe it will stop."
- **Relief** – at finally having a name after months of not knowing. This is normal too.

These feelings come and go, sometimes in one day. They do not mean you love your child any less.

### Grief that begins now

Many parents are surprised to feel grief while their child is alive and beside them. You may be grieving the future you imagined: school, a job, a wedding, being looked after in old age. You may be grieving the child who once ran, talked or drew pictures.

**It is possible to grieve the child you imagined and love the child you have, at the same time.** This kind of grief has a name: some call it *ambiguous loss*, because the loss is real but your child is still here. It does not mean you have given up. We return to this in Chapter 12.

### Hope does not have to end

The doctors may have said there is no cure. That is not the same as saying nothing can be done. You can still hope for:

- good days and comfortable nights,
- less pain, fewer seizures, easier feeding,
- time together, laughter, music and play,
- your child feeling loved and safe.

These hopes are real, and many of them can be reached.

### Telling your family

- **Tell your partner together with the doctor if you can,** so neither of you has to carry the news alone or explain it alone.
- **Ask for a family meeting** if grandparents or other relatives need to understand. Hearing it from the doctor can stop blame and wrong ideas.
- **You do not have to tell everyone at once.** Choose who needs to know now and who can wait.
- **Brothers and sisters need to know too,** in words that fit their age. See Chapter 7.

### The first weeks: small steps

1. Eat something and drink water, even if you are not hungry.
2. Sleep when you can. Take turns with another adult if possible.
3. Choose one person you can talk to honestly.
4. Write down the questions that come to you at night, and take them to the next visit.
5. You do not need to decide everything now.

> [!try]
> On a page of your notebook, write: **three things I now know**, **three questions I still have**, and **one person I can talk to**.

> [!urgent]
> **Get help now** if you have thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, or feel you cannot go on. Tell someone near you, go to the nearest hospital, or call **Tele-MANAS on 14416** (free, 24 hours, in many Indian languages).

# part:2 | Living with the condition

## chapter:4 | Hope, faith and choosing treatments

Almost every parent keeps searching. You may have tried Ayurveda, homeopathy, herbal medicine, a healer, a famous temple or a private hospital that promised more. **You are not wrong for trying.** When you watch your child change in front of your eyes, wanting to try everything is love.

This chapter is not about stopping hope. It is about protecting your child, your money and your strength while you hope.

### Six questions before any new treatment

1. **Who is offering it?** Is this a registered doctor or therapist?
2. **What exactly will it do?** Will it cure, slow the illness, or only ease symptoms?
3. **Has it helped other children with this same condition?** Ask to see the evidence, not just stories.
4. **What will it cost in total,** including travel, stay and lost work?
5. **Can my child continue their present medicines?**
6. **What does my child's own doctor think?** Take the details and ask.

### Warning signs of a false cure

Be very careful if anyone:

- **promises a cure** for a genetic or progressive condition,
- asks you to **stop prescribed medicines,** especially seizure medicines,
- asks for **a large payment in advance,** or quotes lakhs of rupees,
- offers **injections such as "stem cells"** outside an approved research study,
- will not tell you **what is in** the medicine,
- pressures you to **decide quickly,**
- **blames you** if the treatment does not work.

Families have sold land, taken high-interest loans and spent many lakhs on such promises, and their children became worse because proper medicines were stopped. If in doubt, ask the hospital social worker before you pay.

### Keep everyone informed

- **Never stop seizure medicines suddenly.** This can cause dangerous seizures.
- **Tell your doctor about any other remedy** your child is taking, including herbal mixtures and health drinks. Some can affect medicines or special diets.
- **Some conditions do now have specific treatments,** such as enzyme replacement or newer medicines for a few disorders. Ask your doctor whether any apply to your child, and whether rare-disease support can help with the cost (Chapter 8).

### Faith as strength

Many families find strength in prayer, in visits to the temple, church, mosque or dargah, and in their community of faith. Some parents say, "God gave us this, and God will give us the strength to carry it." Faith and medical care can walk together. You can pray **and** give the medicines **and** do the exercises.

It is also normal to ask hard questions: *Why my child? Why us?* Some parents find their beliefs change; others find them deepen. A faith leader you trust, a counsellor or another parent can be good company in these questions.

### Keeping your child safe during rituals

- **During a seizure, remove** tight threads, chains or amulets from the neck and limbs, and move sharp or heavy objects away from the bed and pillow. You can put them back afterwards.
- **Never allow branding, burning or cutting** of the child's skin as a treatment.
- **Fasting or special offerings** should not replace your child's food or medicines.
- If relatives press you to stop hospital treatment, you can say: **"We are doing both: prayer and the doctor's treatment."**

### About the disability card

Some parents do not want a disability certificate (UDID) because they fear the word "disabled" or hope the child will recover soon. Getting the card **does not mean you have given up hope.** It is a key that opens support: pensions, travel concessions and help with school. You can read more in Chapter 8.

> [!try]
> In your notebook, make a **treatment map**: list each treatment you have tried, how much it cost, and what changed for your child. This helps you and your doctor see clearly what helps.

> [!ask]
> - "Is there any treatment for my child's condition, anywhere?"
> - "Is it safe to give this remedy with the present medicines?"
> - "Can you help us check if this clinic is genuine?"

## chapter:5 | Caring for your child day by day

You will learn many new skills: giving medicines on time, helping your child eat safely, moving and positioning their body, watching for seizures. Professionals told us that **parents who are taught well become very skilled carers.** They also told us that parents stop doing a task, or do too much of it, when nobody explains *why* it matters. So in this chapter, every task comes with its reason.

This chapter gives general guidance. **Your child's team will give advice that fits your child.** Always follow their plan.

### Seizures

> [!why]
> knowing what a seizure looks like helps you act quickly and tell the doctor accurately.

Seizures do not always look like shaking. They can be small jerks, a sudden drop of the head, staring and not responding, eyes rolling up, or stiffening of the body.

**During a seizure:**

1. Stay calm and **note the time** it started.
2. Move hard or sharp things away. Remove tight threads or chains from the neck.
3. **Turn your child onto their side** so saliva can drain out.
4. **Do not put anything in the mouth.** Do not hold the body down. Do not give water or food.
5. Stay with your child until they are awake and breathing comfortably.

> [!alert]
> **Call 108 or go to hospital** if a seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes (or the time your doctor has told you), if seizures follow one after another without your child waking in between, if breathing is difficult or the lips turn blue, or if your child is injured. Ask your doctor whether you should keep an emergency medicine at home, and practise how to give it.

Keep a **seizure diary** (in the notebook) and a short phone video if you can. It helps the doctor adjust medicines.

### Eating and drinking

> [!why]
> many of these conditions weaken the muscles for swallowing. Food or drink can go into the lungs and cause chest infections.

- Sit your child **upright** to eat, with head supported and chin slightly down.
- Give **small amounts of soft food,** slowly. Wait for each swallow.
- Keep your child upright for a while after eating.
- **Tell your team** if you notice coughing or choking while eating, a wet or gurgly voice, meals taking much longer, weight loss, or repeated chest infections. A speech and swallowing therapist or dietitian can help, and a feeding tube may make eating safer (see Chapter 11).

**A common mistake is giving too much food.** Many parents think their child is weak because they are not eating enough, so they feed more. The weakness comes from the nerves and muscles, not from hunger. A child who moves very little needs less food, and extra weight makes lifting, breathing and sitting harder. Ask for your child's weight to be checked, and follow the dietitian's plan.

- **Do not give gym protein powders or malted health drinks** unless the dietitian says so. Some can harm the kidneys or upset special diets.
- **If your child is on a special diet** (for example, low-copper food for Wilson's disease, or a ketogenic diet for seizures), follow it exactly. Never change it without asking.

### Medicines

> [!why]
> many medicines only work when the level in the body stays steady.

- Use the **medicines chart** in the notebook. Write each medicine, the dose and the time.
- Give medicines at the **same times every day.** If you miss a dose, ask your doctor or pharmacist what to do. Do not double the next dose unless they tell you to.
- **Never stop or change a medicine on your own,** even if your child seems the same.
- Buy more **before** you run out, especially before travel.
- If your child develops a **new rash or seems unwell after a new medicine,** contact the doctor quickly. **Swelling of the face or difficulty breathing is an emergency.**
- Go back for **regular reviews.** Doses often need to change as children grow.

### Moving, posture and exercise

> [!why]
> exercises do **not** build muscle in these conditions. They keep joints flexible, prevent painful stiffness, and help breathing and comfort.

- Do the exercises your therapist shows you, **gently,** every day.
- **The right amount:** your child should not be tired or in pain the next morning. If they are, you are doing too much. Tell the therapist.
- **Good sitting** matters. A child who slumps to one side can develop a curved back and breathing problems. Ask the occupational therapist about supports. A local carpenter can often build a supportive chair from the therapist's design.
- **If your child cannot move themselves, change their position about every 2 hours,** day and night if possible, to protect the skin.
- **Lifting safely:** bend your knees, keep your child close to your body, and never pull by the arms. Ask someone to help as your child grows heavier. Ask the therapist to show you safe ways to move your child.
- **Splints and braces** help keep joints in a good position and slow down stiffness. They do not cure, and they do not stop recovery.

### Skin, toileting and comfort

- Keep the skin **clean and dry.** Change diapers often. Check the back, hips, heels and elbows every day for red patches that do not fade. Tell the nurse at once if you see broken skin.
- An **air mattress** helps if your child is in bed most of the time.
- For **constipation,** give enough water and fibre-rich food, and ask the team about medicines if needed.
- **Drooling** can often be reduced with treatment. Ask.
- For **girls approaching puberty,** plan early with a nurse how periods will be managed, with privacy and dignity.

### Pain, crying and sleep

Children who cannot speak still feel pain. Watch for crying, a changed face, stiffening, pulling away, not sleeping or not eating. Check for common causes: constipation, hunger, a wet diaper, an uncomfortable position, a sore mouth or loose teeth, or seizures. **Tell your team about long crying spells or poor sleep.** There are treatments that can help your child, and you.

> [!ask]
> - "Can you show me how to do this, and watch me do it once?"
> - "What is an emergency for my child?"
> - "Can we meet the therapist, dietitian or nurse before we go home?"

## chapter:6 | Keeping childhood going

Your child is a child first, and a patient second. Even when the body is changing, your child can still laugh, play, learn, have friends and dream. Professionals told us that parents almost never ask, "Can my child still play?" This chapter asks it for you.

### Play

> [!why]
> play brings joy, keeps the mind active, eases stress and builds closeness. It counts as part of care, not something extra.

- **Adapt the game, not the child.** Board games, carrom on a table, cards, word games, riddles, counting games, colouring, music, stories and songs can all be played sitting or lying down.
- **Use what your child can still do.** If the hands are weak, your child can choose and you can move the pieces. If speech is limited, use pictures, nods or blinks.
- **Brothers and sisters are wonderful play partners.** They are not focused on getting better; they just want to play. Even 30 minutes a day of adapted play together helps both children.
- **Go outside when you can.** A park, a temple courtyard or a walk in a wheelchair lets your child see other children and the world.
- **Screens are fine in moderation,** but a whole day of television or mobile videos leaves a child restless and alone. Mix screen time with play and conversation.

### What brings your child joy?

Parents often notice joys that doctors never see: a favourite song that makes your child dance, a grandparent they adore, a colour, a food, a game. Write them on the **joy page** in your notebook, and share them with the team. They help everyone see your child as a whole person.

### School

> [!why]
> school is not only lessons. It is friends, routine and a sense of belonging.

- **Your child has a right to education.** Under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, schools are expected to make reasonable adjustments.
- **Ask the school for practical changes:** a ground-floor classroom, an accessible toilet, a seat near the door, extra time in exams, permission for a helper, or rest breaks. Moving a classroom downstairs is simple when people are willing.
- **If the school refuses,** ask the hospital social worker for a letter or for help speaking to the school or education office.
- **If your child cannot attend every day,** ask about the National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS), home tutoring, or attending only for exams.
- **Keep friendships going** with visits, phone calls or messages, even when your child is not at school.
- **Plan toileting with dignity.** Many children stop drinking water at school to avoid needing the toilet, which is harmful. Work with the school and nurse on a private plan.

### Talking with your child about the illness

Children understand much more than adults think, often more than they show. Many children **hide their sadness to protect their parents,** just as parents hide theirs to protect the child.

- **Answer questions honestly,** in words that fit your child's age. Short, true answers are better than long explanations.
- **It is all right to say "I don't know"** or "Let's ask the doctor together."
- **Never tell a child something false** about illness or death. False stories can frighten children more than the truth.
- **Let your child talk without fixing.** Sometimes they just need you to listen.
- **Use drawing and stories.** Some children find it easier to draw "me before" and "me now", or to talk about a story character, than to talk about themselves.
- **Let your child keep hoping too.** A boy who wants to be a police officer can still enjoy that dream, through stories, games or a uniform for a day.

### Growing up: teenagers

Teenagers need **privacy,** especially for toileting, bathing and periods. Ask relatives and visitors to step out during personal care. Talk with your teenager about what they want to know and decide. Older teenagers may want to study further or work. Ask the social worker about vocational training or skills that suit your child's abilities.

> [!ask]
> - "Can my child still go to school, and what will help?"
> - "What games or activities suit my child now?"
> - "Can someone help me talk to my child about the illness?"

## chapter:7 | Your family

When one child is ill, the whole family changes. Someone gives up work. Nights are broken. A brother or sister gets less attention. Relatives help, or do not, or say hurtful things. Very often, **most of the load falls on one person, usually the mother.** This chapter is about sharing the load and caring for everyone in the family.

### Between parents

- **Share the care, including at night.** Take turns so each of you gets some unbroken sleep. Even two nights a week makes a difference.
- **Fathers are carers too.** Feeding, bathing, exercises, hospital visits and sitting with the child are not only a mother's work. Children benefit from both parents' hands.
- **Talk about the decisions together** with the doctor, so one parent is not left to explain and carry them.
- **When you start blaming each other,** remember that the illness is the problem, not your partner. Genes are no one's fault (see Chapter 2). A counsellor or social worker can help you talk if arguments grow.
- **Keep a little time for each other,** even 15 minutes of tea together without talking about hospitals.

If you are **raising your child alone,** you are carrying a great deal. Make a list of people who can take small tasks off you, and tell the social worker. Single parents can often get extra support.

### Brothers and sisters

Brothers and sisters often feel worried, confused, lonely, jealous or guilty, and they may hide it. Some take on adult jobs too young. Some are sent to live with relatives. They need honesty and attention too.

| Age | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| Under 6 | Simple words: "Your brother is ill. Doctors are helping him. It is not your fault and you cannot catch it." Keep their routine. Cuddles and play. |
| 6 to 12 | Honest answers to their questions. Let them help in small ways if they want to. Tell them it is all right to feel angry or sad. Tell their teacher. |
| Teenagers | More detail, and include them in family talks. Do not make them a second parent. Respect their need for friends and their own life. |

- **Give each sibling some time alone with you,** even 10 minutes, regularly.
- **Answer questions such as "When will my brother run again?"** with gentle truth: "His illness makes his body weaker. The doctors can't make him run again, but we can help him be comfortable, and you can still play with him in new ways."
- **Never tell them false stories** about illness or death.
- **If a sibling lives with relatives,** keep in touch by phone, visits and photos, and bring them home when you can.
- Ask the team whether a counsellor can meet your other children.

### Grandparents and relatives

Grandparents and relatives can be a great help, or a source of pressure and blame. Many want to help but do not know how. **Give them a clear task:**

- "Please hold her for half an hour so I can eat and sleep."
- "Please cook on Tuesdays."
- "Please stay with him in the ward tonight."

If relatives **blame one parent** or press you to stop treatment, you can say: "The doctor has explained that this comes from genes and is no one's fault. We are following the doctor's advice, and we would like your help." You can also ask the doctor to meet the whole family.

### Neighbours and the community

Some families face staring, mocking or questions. Some neighbours keep their children away because they wrongly fear the illness is catching. Many families stop going to weddings and functions.

- **A short answer is enough:** "It is a medical condition of the nerves. It is not catching, and it is no one's fault."
- **You decide** which functions to attend. It is all right to go, and all right to stay home.
- **Find your people:** a neighbour, a friend, a teacher, a faith group or another parent who understands.

> [!try]
> Fill in **My support circle** in the notebook: who can help with sleep, with money, with the hospital, with your other children, and with listening.

> [!ask]
> - "Can you meet our whole family to explain the condition?"
> - "Is there someone who can talk with our other children?"

## chapter:8 | Money, papers and help

Many families told us that the illness took their savings, their land, their gold and sometimes their jobs. Some borrowed from moneylenders at high monthly interest just to travel to the hospital. Others found that a card or certificate that worked at home was refused somewhere else.

**Help does exist, but it is often hidden behind papers.** This chapter helps you find it. Schemes, amounts and rules change and differ from state to state, so always check the details with the hospital social worker.

### First: find the social worker

Most large government hospitals have a **medical social worker** or social work department. They help families with free medicines, fee waivers, disability certificates, pensions, travel concessions and links to charities. **Ask for them on your first day,** before you pay for anything large.

### Before you pay

- **Ask what a test or treatment will cost,** and whether a free scheme, research funding or charity can cover it.
- **Ask about free medicines** for families with a BPL card or low income.
- **Try not to borrow from moneylenders.** If you already have high-interest debts, tell the social worker. Sometimes charities or relief funds can help.

### Documents to keep ready

Keep originals safe, carry photocopies, and take clear photos on your phone. Use the checklist in the notebook.

- Aadhaar card for your child and for you
- Ration card or BPL card, and income certificate
- **Any online login or user ID** linked to these (some hospitals need it before giving free medicines)
- Your state health card or insurance card
- Disability certificate or UDID card, if you have one
- All medical reports, discharge summaries and prescriptions
- Bank passbook and passport-size photos

### Watch out for these traps

- **State health cards may not work in another state,** or in a central government hospital. Ask the hospital, or the social worker, before you travel.
- **A disability card made in one state** may need to be updated before your home state will pay a pension. Ask the social worker to help before you leave.
- **Missing online IDs** can stop free medicines. Get them sorted at home before travelling if you can.

### The disability card (UDID)

The Unique Disability ID card is a national disability certificate. It can open the door to disability pensions, travel concessions, school support and other schemes. You can apply through the hospital, the district medical board, or online.

- **If your child's condition is not on the official list,** ask the doctor. Certification can be based on what your child is unable to do, not only on the name of the condition.
- **Getting the card does not mean giving up hope.** It simply makes support easier to reach.

### Other help to ask about

- **Disability pension** from the state government. Amounts vary and are often small, and payments can be delayed; keep a record and contact your panchayat or municipal office if they stop.
- **Rare disease support.** Under the National Policy for Rare Diseases, some listed conditions can receive help with treatment through Centres of Excellence. Ask your doctor whether your child's condition is listed and how to register.
- **Health insurance:** Ayushman Bharat (PM-JAY) and state schemes cover hospital stays for eligible families. The Niramaya scheme offers health insurance for some disabilities. Ask whether your child qualifies.
- **Cheaper medicines:** Jan Aushadhi stores sell generic medicines at lower cost. Ask your doctor if your child's medicines are available there.
- **Equipment:** wheelchairs, special chairs and air mattresses may be available through government camps, charities or device banks. **Ask for a proper assessment first,** so the equipment fits your child. A wheelchair that is too narrow is of little use.
- **Charities and community organisations** often help faster than government schemes with food kits, beds, medicines or travel costs.

### Work

If hospital visits mean many days away from work, ask the hospital for a letter for your employer explaining your child's needs. Talk to your employer before you resign; there may be other options. If one parent has had to stop work, Chapter 10 talks about finding a way back to earning later.

> [!ask]
> - "Can I meet the social worker today?"
> - "Is this test or medicine free under any scheme?"
> - "Which documents will I need for the pension and the disability card?"

## chapter:9 | Your care team and care plan

In the big hospital, many people look after your child. Then you go home, sometimes hundreds of kilometres away, and suddenly there is no one to ask. Many families told us this is when they feel most lost. **A good plan before you leave, and one person you can call, make a great difference.**

### Who is in the team

Your child's team may include a neurologist or paediatrician, a palliative or supportive care team, nurses, a social worker, a physiotherapist, an occupational therapist, a speech and swallowing therapist, a dietitian, a psychologist or counsellor, and closer to home, your local doctor, the Primary Health Centre and the ASHA worker. **You and your family are part of this team.** You know your child best.

### One person to call

Ask the hospital: **"Who is our main contact after we go home, and how do we reach them?"** Write the name and number on the emergency card in your notebook. This person can help when you are confused, when new symptoms appear, or when someone offers you a new "cure".

### Before you leave hospital: your care plan

Ask for these, and keep them in your folder:

- [ ] A short written summary of the diagnosis, in simple words
- [ ] The list of medicines, doses and times
- [ ] What to watch for, and what counts as an emergency
- [ ] What to do in an emergency, and which hospital near home to go to
- [ ] Exercises and care tasks, ideally practised once in front of the team
- [ ] The next review date, and whether it can be done by phone or video
- [ ] A letter for your local doctor
- [ ] Contacts for therapy, palliative care or support near your home

### Care closer to home

- **Local doctors are sometimes afraid to treat** a child with a big-hospital diagnosis, and send you back for small problems. A letter from your child's specialist explaining what the local doctor can safely manage, and how to reach the specialist, often helps.
- **The Primary Health Centre and ASHA worker** may be able to help with home visits, medicines or referrals. Introduce your child to them.
- **Ask about phone or video follow-up,** so you do not have to travel for every review.
- **Ask whether a home-care or palliative team covers your area.** Many cover only nearby places, but they may know a team closer to you.

### When you see a new doctor

Carry your folder and the **"My child"** page from the notebook. It saves you from explaining everything again, especially in an emergency.

### Other parents

Talking to parents who have walked the same road can bring comfort, practical tips and friendship. Many parents say they felt less alone after joining a group.

**Choosing a group wisely:**

- Groups for **the same or similar condition** are usually most helpful. Comparing your child with a child who has a very different illness can cause distress.
- Groups linked to a **hospital, a professional or a known patient organisation** are more likely to share reliable information.
- **Check advice with your team** before trying anything you hear about in a group or online.
- **Protect your privacy.** Think before sharing your phone number, address or your child's photos in open groups.
- It is fine to take a break from a group if it makes you feel worse.

> [!ask]
> - "Who is our main contact after discharge?"
> - "Can you write a letter for our local doctor?"
> - "Is there a parent group for my child's condition?"

# part:3 | Caring for yourself

## chapter:10 | Your body, your mind, your life

This may be the chapter you skip, because there is no time for yourself. Please read it anyway. **Your child needs you, and you cannot pour from an empty vessel.** If you fall ill or break down, who will care for your child? Caring for yourself is part of caring for your child. It is not selfish.

### Sleep

Many parents told us they sleep only a few hours, waking again and again, listening for breathing, seizures or crying. Some carry their child all night.

- **Take turns at night** with your partner or another adult, so each person gets some unbroken sleep.
- **Ask one relative or friend for one night a week,** or even a few hours, so you can sleep deeply.
- **Rest when your child rests,** even for 20 minutes.
- **Tell the doctor about your child's night problems.** Night seizures, pain, breathing difficulty or constant waking can often be treated, and better nights for your child mean better nights for you.

### Your body

- **Go for your own check-ups.** Many carers develop diabetes, high blood pressure, back or shoulder injuries and headaches. Do not ignore new pain, bleeding or illness in yourself.
- **Lift safely** (Chapter 5) and ask for help as your child grows heavier. Ask the therapist about equipment that makes lifting easier.
- **Eat regular meals.** Even simple food at regular times keeps you going.

### Your feelings

You may feel exhausted, sad, numb, angry, guilty, lonely or hopeless, sometimes all in one day. Some parents feel a deep sorrow that does not lift. Some feel nothing at all for a while. **These feelings are a normal response to an extremely hard situation.**

**Feeling angry with your child, your partner or God does not make you a bad parent.** When you have not slept for days and your child has cried for two hours, anger can rise. What matters is what you do with it:

1. Put your child somewhere safe, such as their bed.
2. Step away for a few minutes.
3. Breathe slowly: in for a count of 4, out for a count of 6, ten times.
4. Call someone, or ask someone to take over.
5. **Never shake or hit a child,** however exhausted you are. If you are afraid you might, ask for help today.

### Two simple ways to calm your body

- **Slow breathing:** sit comfortably, breathe in through the nose for 4 counts, out through the mouth for 6 counts. Repeat for 2 to 3 minutes.
- **Tense and release:** starting with your feet, tighten the muscles for 5 seconds, then let go and notice the relaxation. Move up through legs, stomach, hands, arms, shoulders and face. This takes about 10 minutes and can help you fall asleep.

### Time that is not about caring

When every moment with your child is about medicines, feeding and cleaning, you can start to feel like a nurse rather than a parent. Each day, try to spend **a few minutes with your child that is not about care:** singing a song you both love, telling a story, sitting outside, looking at photos together.

And try to keep **15 minutes a day for yourself:** a walk, prayer, music, a phone call with a friend, a cup of tea in peace.

### Your own life and future

Many mothers leave college or work to care for their child. Some fathers lose jobs. Your own dreams still matter. When things are steadier, ask the social worker about courses, skills training or work you can do from home. Some parents have found new strength in work, study or helping other families.

### Asking for help

People often say, "Tell me if you need anything." Give them a real task: an hour of care, a meal, a hospital trip, a bill. Ask the team about short breaks (respite), where someone trained looks after your child for a few hours or days.

### Warning signs: get help now

Talk to a doctor, counsellor or the social worker soon if, for two weeks or more, you:

- cannot sleep even when you have the chance, or sleep all the time,
- have stopped eating, or cry most days,
- feel numb, hopeless or worthless,
- are using alcohol or tablets to cope.

> [!urgent]
> **If you have thoughts of harming yourself, ending your life, or harming your child,** tell someone near you right away, go to the nearest hospital, or call **Tele-MANAS on 14416** (free, 24 hours, in many Indian languages). Asking for help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

Use the **weekly self-care check** in the notebook to keep an eye on yourself.

# part:4 | When the illness advances

## chapter:11 | Changes as the illness progresses

In progressive conditions, things your child once did may slowly become harder. One mother told us her daughter used to draw for hours, dress herself and go to school alone; now she rubs her stomach to say she is hungry. Every child's path is different, and some change slowly over many years. This chapter helps you recognise changes, make decisions, and keep your child comfortable.

### Changes you may see

Not every child will have all of these:

- walking becomes harder, then sitting without support
- speech becomes slower or stops
- chewing and swallowing become difficult
- seizures become more frequent or change
- breathing weakens, with more chest infections
- muscles become stiff, with pain or curving of the back
- changes in sight, hearing, sleep or behaviour, including crying spells or restlessness
- loss of bladder and bowel control

**Tell your team when you notice a change.** Many changes can be eased even when the illness cannot be stopped.

### Understanding a child who cannot speak

Your child still has things to say. You will become the expert in their language.

- **Learn their signals.** Write down what each gesture, sound or look means, such as rubbing the stomach for hunger, turning away for "no", or a smile for "more".
- **Make a yes / no system:** a blink, a look up, a hand squeeze.
- **Use pictures.** Simple cards (food, water, pain, toilet, mother, music) let your child point or look. A speech therapist can help you make them.
- **Share the list** with everyone who cares for your child, so your child is understood even when you are not there.

When a child cannot make themselves understood, they may scream or cry in frustration. That is communication too.

### Decisions about feeding tubes

When swallowing becomes unsafe, the team may suggest a feeding tube. This is one of the hardest decisions for many parents.

- **What it is for:** safer nutrition and medicines, fewer chest infections from food going into the lungs, and calmer, shorter mealtimes.
- **Types:** a thin tube through the nose (NG or Ryle's tube), usually for short-term use; or a tube placed into the stomach through the tummy (PEG), for longer use. A PEG is hidden under clothes, and many families find it more comfortable.
- **It is not giving up.** Many children with a tube live more comfortably and gain strength. Some can still enjoy small tastes of favourite food by mouth if the therapist says it is safe.
- **Earlier is often easier.** Nurses told us children who had a tube placed while still relatively well did better than those who waited until they were very weak. Ask your team about timing.
- **Plan for home:** the team will teach you how to feed through the tube and keep it clean. Ask who near your home can help if the tube comes out or blocks.

### Breathing

> [!soft]
> Watch for fast or noisy breathing, struggling to breathe, blue or grey lips, very heavy sleepiness, or repeated chest infections. **Breathing difficulty is an emergency: call 108 or go to the hospital.**

Ask your team about positions that ease breathing, how to clear secretions, and whether a breathing machine at night or other equipment could help your child.

### Comfort comes first

As the illness advances, the goal of care slowly shifts: from trying to change the course of the illness, to keeping your child as **comfortable, calm and pain-free** as possible.

- **Pain and breathlessness can be treated.** Medicines such as morphine, prescribed by a trained doctor, are safe and effective for children when used correctly. Fear of these medicines should not keep your child in pain. Ask about them.
- **For any treatment, you can ask:** "Will this help my child feel better, or will it mainly make things harder for them?" Good teams welcome this question.
- **Small pleasures matter.** If your child's diet or routine is restricted, talk with the team about how to balance safety with your child's happiness, such as a favourite taste or an outing.

> [!ask]
> - "What changes should we expect next?"
> - "Is a feeding tube right for my child, and when?"
> - "What can we do to keep our child comfortable at home?"

## chapter:12 | Grieving while your child is alive

Each time your child loses a skill, you may feel a new wave of loss: the first time they cannot walk, cannot say your name, cannot eat their favourite food. Some mothers told us they feel no happiness left in their home. Another said that seeing healthy children hurts so much that she stopped going out.

This is grief, even though your child is still here. It has many names: anticipatory grief, ambiguous loss, chronic sorrow. **It is not a sign that you have given up, and it is not wrong.** It is love, facing loss.

### What it can feel like

- Waves of sadness that come without warning, then pass
- Pain on birthdays, festivals, school reopening days, or when you see other children
- Feeling guilty for grieving while your child is alive
- Feeling numb, or busy all the time so you do not have to feel
- Feeling relief on good days, then fear of the next change

### Hiding feelings from each other

Many parents hide their sadness so their child will not see it. Many children, especially older ones who understand their illness, hide their own fear and sadness to protect their parents. Everyone ends up alone with the same feelings.

- **It is all right to be sad together.** You can say: "I feel sad sometimes too. We can be sad and still love each other and have good times."
- **Let your child talk** about what they have lost and what they fear, without rushing to cheer them up.
- **Ask the team for a counsellor or psychologist** if your child seems withdrawn, very angry, or speaks about being a burden or being useless. Children can be helped with these feelings.

### When partners grieve differently

One parent may cry and want to talk; the other may go quiet or work harder. Neither is wrong. Try to tell each other what helps you, and what does not.

### If you have lost a child before

Some families have already lost a child to the same illness, and now watch another child walk the same road. This is one of the heaviest burdens any parent can carry. Old grief and new fear mix together. **Please tell your team about your earlier loss,** and ask for a counsellor. You deserve extra support.

### Finding meaning

Many parents find a way to live alongside grief. Some draw on faith. Some decide that, for as long as their child is here, they will fill each day with love. Some find meaning in helping other families. There is no single right way.

Things that help some families:

- **Mark good days.** Write one good moment each day in the notebook: a smile, a song, a calm night.
- **Take photos and short videos now,** of ordinary moments, not only special ones.
- **Keep small rituals of joy:** a favourite song every evening, a story, oil massage, sitting in the sun together.
- **Let yourself limit** visits or events that hurt too much, for now.

### When grief is too heavy

> [!soft]
> If, for weeks, you cannot sleep or eat, feel hopeless, cannot care for yourself, or think about not wanting to live, please ask for help (see Chapter 10). **Tele-MANAS: 14416, free, 24 hours.**

> [!ask]
> - "Can we talk to a counsellor about how we are feeling?"
> - "Can someone talk with our child about their worries?"

# part:5 | Towards the end, and after

## chapter:13 | Preparing for the end

This is the chapter no parent wants to read. You may choose to read it now, later, or with someone beside you. Families and professionals told us that **when parents are prepared, their child's last days can be calmer, more comfortable and more full of love,** and parents carry less regret afterwards.

Every child is different. Some children live for many years with their condition. Your team can tell you whether this chapter is relevant now.

### Talking with the team: planning ahead

This is sometimes called **advance care planning**. It means talking, before a crisis, about what matters most for your child and family. You can ask for a family meeting.

Questions you might talk about:

- What is most important for our child now: comfort, time at home, being with family, avoiding hospital?
- **Where would we like our child to be** when they are very ill: at home, in hospital, or in a palliative care centre?
- **Which treatments would help, and which would mainly cause discomfort without changing what is happening?** For example, intensive care or a breathing machine. Ask the doctor to explain what each would mean for your child.
- Whom do we call at night, and what do we do if our child suddenly becomes very unwell?

You can change your mind at any time. Write down what you decide, keep it with your folder, and make sure everyone caring for your child knows.

### Keeping your child comfortable

- **Pain, breathlessness, restlessness and secretions can all be eased** with the right medicines and care. Ask the palliative team for a plan, and for medicines to keep at home.
- **Mouth care:** keep lips and mouth moist, even if your child is not drinking.
- **Positioning:** change position gently and keep your child warm and comfortable.
- **Presence:** familiar voices, touch, favourite songs, prayers and stories. Your child may hear you even when they cannot respond.

### Making memories

Many families told us that making memories together helped them, and their other children, later. You might:

- make **hand and foot prints** with paint or clay,
- take **photos and videos,** and record your child's voice or laughter,
- keep a **lock of hair,** a favourite piece of clothing or a toy,
- create something together: a painting, a song, a story, a family photo,
- celebrate a birthday or festival early, if you wish,
- make a **memory box** to keep these things.

Involve brothers and sisters if they want to take part.

### Faith and rituals

Prayers, blessings and rituals can bring great comfort. Tell the team what your family would like, such as a priest, imam, pastor or elder to visit, or prayers at certain times. Good teams will respect these wishes.

### Preparing brothers and sisters

- Tell them honestly, in words they can understand, that their brother or sister is very ill and may die soon.
- **Do not use words that confuse,** such as "going to sleep" or "going on a journey", especially with young children. They may become afraid of sleeping or travelling.
- Let them say goodbye in their own way: a drawing, a letter, a song, holding hands. Let them choose whether to be present.

### The last days and hours

Your team can help you recognise when the end may be near. Common changes include sleeping much more, eating and drinking very little, breathing changes, and cool hands and feet. These are usually not painful for the child, but ask the team about anything that worries you.

Stay close. Talk, hold, sing, pray. Some parents tell their child it is all right to rest. **Whatever happens in those moments, you have loved and cared for your child with everything you had.**

### Practical matters

Ask the social worker in advance about what happens after death: certificates, transport home, and any support available. Hospital or palliative staff can help with washing and dressing your child. You may want to do this yourself, or with them, or not at all. All are fine.

> [!ask]
> - "Can we have a family meeting about planning ahead?"
> - "What medicines should we keep at home for comfort?"
> - "Whom do we call at night?"

## chapter:14 | After your child has died

There are no words that make this loss smaller. This chapter is here for whenever you need it: in the first days, months later, or years later.

### Grief has no timetable

Grief after a child's death can feel like a heavy weight on the chest, a fog, exhaustion, anger, numbness or deep longing. It may come in waves for a long time. There is no correct way or length of time to grieve.

- **The silence can be the hardest part.** Your days were built around care: medicines, feeding, turning, watching. When that stops, the emptiness can be overwhelming.
- **Relief and guilt can arrive together.** You may feel relief that your child is no longer suffering, or that the exhausting care has ended, and then feel guilty for that relief. Both feelings are normal and come from love.
- **"If only..." thoughts are very common.** "If only I had noticed sooner," "if only I had followed the advice exactly." Please remember: these illnesses progress regardless of how much a parent does. **You cared for your child with everything you had.**

### Staying connected to the team

Many palliative teams continue to support families after a child's death. You can ask to talk with the doctor, nurse, counsellor or social worker who knew your child, and ask questions you were not able to ask before. Some families find it helps to return, to thank the team, or simply to talk about their child with people who knew them.

### Brothers and sisters

Children grieve differently from adults. They may seem fine one moment and very upset the next. They may show grief through play, anger, clinginess, trouble at school or stomach aches.

- **Tell them the truth** about the death, in simple words. Avoid saying the child "became a star" or "is sleeping". Young children may take these words literally, with frightening results.
- **Keep them close.** Try not to send them away for long periods, and keep daily routines going where you can.
- **They may feel guilty,** or relieved that the household is calmer, or that they are no longer second. Reassure them that nothing they thought or did caused the death.
- **Do not ask them to replace** their brother or sister, or to be "strong" for you.
- **Watch for signs they need more help:** long-lasting sadness, withdrawal, anger, school refusal, or talk of wanting to be with the child who died. Ask for a counsellor.

### Remembering

- Keep your child's memory box, photos and videos.
- Mark birthdays and anniversaries in ways that feel right to your family: a prayer, a meal, lighting a lamp, giving to others.
- Talk about your child by name. Many parents want others to keep remembering too.

### Partners and family

You and your partner may grieve in very different ways and at different times. Be patient with each other. If blame or distance grows, a counsellor can help. Relatives may not know what to say. You can tell them: "Please just say her name, and listen."

### The future

- **Going back to work or study** can bring both comfort and pain. Take it step by step. The social worker can help with training or livelihood support.
- **Thinking about another pregnancy?** Please meet a **genetic counsellor first,** or as early as possible in the pregnancy. They can explain the chances of the condition happening again and what tests are possible. Tests to check for the condition are allowed, but telling parents the sex of an unborn baby is not permitted by Indian law. Fear during another pregnancy is very natural. Ask for support.
- **Other children in the family** may also need testing. Ask the genetic counsellor.
- **Some parents later find meaning in helping other families** by joining or starting a parent group, or by sharing what they learned. Only you will know if and when that feels right.

### When grief is too heavy

> [!soft]
> If months pass and you still cannot sleep, eat, work or care for your other children, feel hopeless, or think about ending your life, please reach out. **Tele-MANAS: 14416, free, 24 hours, in many Indian languages.** You do not have to carry this alone.

> [!ask]
> - "Can we meet you to talk about our child?"
> - "Is there a counsellor for us and our other children?"
> - "Can we see a genetic counsellor before another pregnancy?"
